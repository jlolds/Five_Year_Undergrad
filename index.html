The American Plan

A War Game: Write a Five-Year Plan for American Science, 2027–2031

Scenario

JANUARY 2027. China is in Year Two of its 15th Five-Year Plan. It has named its priority technologies, set its targets, and told every ministry, province, and university what is expected of them through 2030. In Washington, a bipartisan group asks an uncomfortable question: Should America have one too?

You have been appointed to a National Science Planning Commission. Your job is to write the first Five-Year Plan for American Science, covering 2027 through 2031.

There is a catch. America was not built to do this. Congress appropriates money one year at a time. A new President can be inaugurated in January 2029, halfway through your plan. Universities, companies, and states make their own choices. And many scientists believe the best discoveries are the ones nobody planned.

The whole game in one sentence: Write a plan on one page, then find out in Year 3 whether it survived contact with America.

The Plan Fits on One Page

Every room fills in the same five boxes. Nothing more.

BoxWhat goes in it
1. The GoalOne sentence. What should American science be able to do in 2031 that it cannot do today?
2. Three PrioritiesThree fields or missions, ranked 1, 2, 3. A ranking means #3 loses if money is short.
3. The CutOne real thing the federal government stops or shrinks to pay for the priorities. "Waste" and "fraud" are not allowed answers.
4. The TargetOne measurable number with a deadline. Example form: "X by 2031." Someone must be able to check it.
5. The EnforcerWho makes the plan stick, and how, when Congress votes every year and the White House can change hands?

Ground rules. No dice, no points, no scoring. The instructor decides what happens to your plan, based on how well you defended it. A plan you cannot defend in two minutes is not a plan.

How China Plans

The People's Republic has issued Five-Year Plans since 1953. The current one, the 15th Five-Year Plan (2026–2030), was approved by the National People's Congress on March 12, 2026.

How a Plan Gets Made

  1. The Party sets direction. The Communist Party's Central Committee issues "Recommendations" (for the 15th Plan, in October 2025).
  2. The planners write the Outline. The National Development and Reform Commission drafts a long document with goals and numerical targets.
  3. The legislature approves it. The National People's Congress votes it through in March.
  4. Everyone else writes a plan underneath it. The Outline sits on top of dozens of sector plans, provincial plans, and ministry plans. A province's plan for science must fit the national one.
  5. Officials are judged against it. Meeting targets helps careers. Missing them hurts.

What the 15th Plan Says About Science

  • Self-reliance in science and technology is the headline goal, especially where China depends on foreign suppliers (chips are the obvious example).
  • Named frontier fields include quantum technology, 6G, fusion and hydrogen energy, biomanufacturing, brain-computer interfaces, and "embodied AI" (robots).
  • More basic research. China has said for two plans running that it wants a larger share of R&D spending to go to basic research, a long-standing weakness.
Why it works (when it works): The same Party will be in charge in 2030 that wrote the plan in 2025. Targets reach every level of government. Money, people, and land can be pointed in one direction for years.
Why it fails (when it fails): People hit the number instead of the goal. For years Chinese universities paid cash bonuses for papers in indexed journals; paper counts soared and so did low-quality and fraudulent work. In 2020 the government told universities to stop rewarding paper counts. A target is a promise that people will learn to game.

Does America Already Plan? Sort Of.

American "plan"What it isWhat it lacks
Vannevar Bush, Science, the Endless Frontier (1945)The founding vision: fund basic research, let scientists chooseTargets. Bush argued against them.
National Academies decadal surveys (astronomy, planetary science, others)Scientists rank their own field's priorities for ten yearsMoney. Congress can ignore them.
CHIPS and Science Act (2022)A law that named key technology areas and authorized big increases for NSFAppropriations. Much of the authorized money was never appropriated.
Annual White House R&D priorities memoTells agencies what to emphasize in their budget requestsDurability. It changes every year and with every President.

Rooms & Roles

There are three breakout rooms. Every room writes the same one-page plan. The difference is who created your Commission, which decides what power you have.

Room A: The White House Commission

Created by: Executive Order.

Your power: You can direct federal agencies (NSF, NIH, DOE, NASA) to reorganize their priorities. You can move fast.

Your weakness: You cannot appropriate a single dollar. An Executive Order can be cancelled by the next President with a signature.

Room B: The Congressional Commission

Created by: Statute, passed by both houses.

Your power: Your plan has the force of law and Congress holds the money.

Your weakness: 535 members, every one with a home district. Every priority will be asked: which states get the money? And next year's Congress can rewrite this year's law.

Room C: The National Academies Commission

Created by: The scientific community itself, like a decadal survey.

Your power: Legitimacy. Scientists trust you because you are them.

Your weakness: No legal authority and no money. Your plan is a recommendation. You must persuade people who do not have to listen.

Move 1: Write the Plan (17 minutes)

JANUARY 2027. Your Commission meets for the first and only time before the plan is due. You have 17 minutes. Fill in all five boxes.

Your Task

  1. The Goal. One sentence.
  2. Three Priorities, ranked. Not five. Three. And #3 is the one you would sacrifice.
  3. The Cut. Name a real program, field, or practice that shrinks. Be ready to say who will be angry.
  4. The Target. One number, one date. Then ask: how would someone cheat to hit it?
  5. The Enforcer. Given your room's power and weakness, who makes this stick until 2031?
Suggested pace: 3 min on the Goal, 5 min on Priorities, 3 min on the Cut, 3 min on the Target, 3 min on the Enforcer. If you are stuck on the Goal at minute 6, the Chair picks one.
What makes a plan strong: Priorities that actually exclude things. A Cut that hurts someone real. A Target that would be hard to fake. An Enforcer that matches your room's actual power.

Report-Out 1 (2 minutes per room)

The Reporter reads the five boxes. The instructor asks one question. The class then hears from the next room. Listen carefully: in Move 2 you will learn what happened to your plan, and comparing it with the others is the point.

Move 2: The Year 3 Review (10 minutes)

JANUARY 2029. Two years have passed. A new President has been inaugurated and Congress has turned over. Your Commission is called back for its mid-term review.

Every Room Receives This

THE SURPRISE. The biggest scientific breakthrough of 2028 came from a field none of the three plans named as a priority. The lab that made it was funded by a small, unglamorous basic-research grant, the kind of grant that plans tend to shrink. Reporters are asking every Commission the same question: "Would your plan have killed this?"

Your Room Also Receives One of These

Room A (White House): Rescinded

On her first afternoon in office, the new President cancels the Executive Order that created your Commission. Agencies are told to "return to normal processes." Two agencies quietly keep following your plan anyway. Three stop immediately. Your plan now exists only where people chose to keep it.

Room B (Congress): The Target Was Hit

Good news: your Target is on track. Bad news: the Government Accountability Office reports that most of the progress came from relabeling existing work to count toward the Target, not from new results. Members from states that won the money are delighted. Members from states that lost it want the law repealed.

Room C (Academies): Applauded and Ignored

Your plan was praised in Science and Nature, cited in hundreds of papers, and followed by almost no one with money. Appropriators funded what they always fund. One foreign government, however, has adopted your priority list almost word for word.

Your Task

  1. Keep, Bend, or Break? Decide whether the plan survives as written, survives with changes, or should be abandoned.
  2. Change exactly ONE box. You may rewrite only one of the five boxes. Choose carefully. Which box failed?
  3. Answer the reporters. One sentence: would your plan have killed the 2028 breakthrough?
  4. The verdict. One sentence: "After Year 3, we believe America (can / cannot) plan science because..."

Report-Out 2 (2 minutes per room)

The Reporter states Keep, Bend, or Break, names the box you changed and why, and reads the verdict.

Debrief

The core question: China's plan survives five years because the people who wrote it will still be in charge when it ends. What, if anything, plays that role in America?

Discussion Questions

  • Which box was hardest? Most rooms find the Cut hardest. Why is it so much easier to name priorities than to name what loses?
  • Power vs. legitimacy. Room A had speed, Room B had money and law, Room C had trust. Whose plan did best in Year 3? What does that tell you about where durable power over science sits in America?
  • Goodhart's problem. Every Target can be gamed. China learned this with paper counts. Did any room write a Target that could not be faked? Is that even possible in science?
  • The surprise. Would any plan have funded the 2028 breakthrough? If not, should a plan deliberately protect money for things nobody can plan?
  • The Skeptic's case. Vannevar Bush built the Miracle Machine on the idea that you fund good people and let them choose. Is a Five-Year Plan a betrayal of that idea, or the overdue next step?
  • The hidden plan. Decadal surveys, CHIPS and Science, the annual priorities memo. Does America already plan science, just badly? Or is a decentralized system its own kind of plan?
One-line exit ticket: If you could make only one part of China's planning system American, which part would you take, and what would it cost?
